## Background

Every IBM Quantum&reg; QPU reports a set of properties that describe the current performance of its qubits: relaxation times ($T_1$), coherence times ($T_2$), readout errors, and one- and two-qubit gate errors. These numbers matter in practice. The transpiler uses them to select the physical qubits for your circuit, error mitigation techniques rely on them, and you might use them to judge whether a device is working well enough for an experiment.

The reported properties come from calibration procedures that typically run about once a day. Superconducting qubits, however, can drift on shorter timescales, and transient defects (known as two-level systems) can temporarily degrade a qubit that performed well at calibration time. In addition, you often transpile a job well before it runs, so decisions based on the reported properties can depend on outdated information.

This tutorial uses the `BackendCharacterization` utility from [Qiskit Device Benchmarking](https://github.com/qiskit-community/qiskit-device-benchmarking), which combines experiment construction, job submission, and curve fitting into a few calls. For roughly 70 seconds of QPU time, you receive a backend object whose properties reflect the current state of the device. You can compare these properties against the reported values or pass the backend to other tools. The final example uses the refreshed values to select physical qubits for a GHZ-state circuit and compares the results with those of the same circuit compiled with the reported properties.

### The characterization experiments

The suite measures five properties, each with a standard experiment:

- **Readout error** (`readout`): The experiment prepares each qubit in $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ and immediately measures it. The probability of a wrong readout gives the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) error.
- **Single-qubit gate error** (`rb_1q`): Randomized benchmarking (RB) runs sequences of random single-qubit Clifford gates that ideally compose to the identity. The decay of the survival probability with sequence length gives the average error per gate, independent of SPAM errors.
- **Two-qubit gate error** (`rb_2q`): This experiment applies the same RB method to full layers of non-overlapping two-qubit gates that run simultaneously, as in a [layer fidelity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05933) experiment. Because many gates run at once, the resulting error rates include crosstalk effects and are closer to the error rates in a deep circuit.
- **$T_1$** (`t1`): The experiment excites each qubit and measures it after increasing delays. The decay of the excited-state population gives the relaxation time.
- **$T_2$** (`t2`): A Hahn echo experiment puts each qubit in a superposition, applies an echo pulse halfway through a delay, and measures how quickly the qubit loses phase coherence. By default, the experiment uses a single echo. A later section of this tutorial demonstrates a multi-echo (CPMG-style) variant.

### Scope and limits of the refreshed properties

Keep the following points in mind when you use this workflow:

- The refreshed properties exist only in the backend object that the utility returns to you. Nothing changes on the device itself or in the calibration data that IBM Quantum reports to other users.
- The measured values are a snapshot taken with a particular methodology. Some of them, notably the layered two-qubit errors and the parallel single-echo $T_2$ values, are measured differently from the reported calibration data, so part of any gap that you observe comes from methodology rather than drift. This tutorial identifies these cases.
- The characterization itself costs QPU time (about 70 seconds for the full suite on a 156-qubit Heron processor), which is the cost of current information.

The workflow follows the four steps of a Qiskit pattern:

- **Step 1: Map classical inputs to a quantum problem.** Choose the device and the set of properties to measure.
- **Step 2: Optimize problem for quantum hardware execution.** The utility builds the experiment circuits directly in the native gates of the device and parallelizes them across the device.
- **Step 3: Execute using Qiskit primitives.** The experiments run as Sampler jobs inside a batch.
- **Step 4: Post-process and return result in desired classical format.** Fit the measurement data into per-qubit error maps, refresh the backend, and compare the measured properties against the reported ones.

## Small-scale simulator example

This tutorial does not include a simulator example. The workflow measures the physical imperfections of a specific device: how quickly its qubits relax and dephase, and how often its gates and readout fail. An ideal simulator has none of these imperfections, so there is nothing to characterize. If you attach a synthetic noise model to a fake backend, the experiments only recover the values that you put in the model. For that reason, this tutorial proceeds directly to hardware, in the four steps of a Qiskit pattern.
## Large-scale hardware example

### Step 1: Map classical inputs to a quantum problem

In this workflow, the "problem" is the device itself. The classical inputs are the QPU that you want to characterize and the list of properties to measure. The quantum experiments are the characterization circuits that the utility generates from these inputs.

First, select the least-busy available QPU with a minimum of 120 qubits. The results in this tutorial were collected on `ibm_boston`, a Heron r3 processor, on 6 October 2026.

```python
service = QiskitRuntimeService()
backend = service.least_busy(
    operational=True,
    simulator=False,
    min_num_qubits=120,
)
```

Next, choose which properties to measure. You can pass any subset of the following experiments:

- `readout`: SPAM (state preparation and measurement) experiment
- `rb_1q`: isolated single-qubit randomized benchmarking
- `rb_2q`: simultaneous two-qubit randomized benchmarking from a layer fidelity experiment
- `t2`: $T_2$ Hahn echo experiment (a single echo by default)
- `t1`: $T_1$ relaxation experiment

Run the full suite to characterize the device completely. To save QPU time, remove the entries for any properties that you do not need.

```python
experiments = ["readout", "rb_1q", "rb_2q", "t1", "t2"]
```

### Step 2: Optimize problem for quantum hardware execution

In most tutorials, this step is where you transpile your circuits. In this tutorial, `BackendCharacterization` handles transpilation internally. It builds the experiment circuits directly in the native gates of the device, runs the single-qubit experiments on all qubits in parallel, and schedules the two-qubit benchmarking over disjoint layers of the coupling map, so that a small number of jobs cover the entire device. Because of this parallelization, the whole suite uses only about 70 seconds of QPU time.

Initialize the class that manages the workflow:

```python
# Initialize the class used to run the experiments
characterizer = BackendCharacterization(backend)
```

Run it inside a [`Batch`](https://docs.quantum.ibm.com/ibm-quantum-compute/run-jobs-batch) context so that the jobs are scheduled together on the QPU. The method returns after it submits all of the jobs. You can also run the method outside a `Batch` or inside a `Session`.

### Step 4: Post-process and return result in desired classical format

#### Analyze the results

The `analyze_results` method waits for the jobs to finish and then processes the measurement data. It fits exponential decays to the $T_1$ and $T_2$ data, fits survival-probability decays to the RB data, and computes assignment matrices for readout. It returns a dictionary of *error maps*, with one entry per measured property. Each error map assigns a measured value to each qubit (or qubit pair).

```python
error_maps = characterizer.analyze_results()
```

Read these plots carefully, because not every gap between the two curves means that the device has drifted:

- **Readout and single-qubit errors**: The measured values track the reported ones for most qubits, but for a few qubits the reported value is far from the measured curve. These localized disagreements can reflect the drift that this workflow is designed to detect.
- **$T_1$**: The two data sets scatter around each other with no systematic offset, which is consistent with natural fluctuation of $T_1$ over time.
- **Two-qubit errors**: The measured values are systematically above the reported ones. #### Measure T2 with a dynamical decoupling train

By default, the $T_2$ experiment applies a single Hahn echo and measures all qubits in parallel. As the previous comparison plot shows, this method can produce noticeably lower $T_2$ values than the backend reports. One possible cause is the number of echoes. The reported values might come from a calibration that uses dynamical decoupling, which suppresses low-frequency noise. To test this hypothesis, run a $T_2$-only characterization with a larger value of `t2_num_echoes`, which applies a CPMG-style train of echo pulses. In both cases, the delays represent the total free-evolution time, so the fitted $T_2$ values are directly comparable to the single-echo results.

```python
# Run a T2-only characterization using a CPMG-style train of 8 echoes
characterizer_dd = BackendCharacterization(backend)
backend_updated_dd = characterizer_dd.run_and_update(
    experiments=["t2"], t2_num_echoes=8
)
```

## Use refreshed properties for qubit selection

This optional example completes the workflow. It checks whether the refreshed properties lead to better circuit results. It compiles the same circuits twice, once with the reported properties and once with the refreshed properties. Then it runs both versions and compares their outputs. This example uses the `backend` and `backend_updated` objects from the previous sections, and it submits additional work to the QPU.

### Build the comparison circuits

Prepare an $n$-qubit Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state with a Hadamard gate followed by a chain of CNOT gates, and measure every qubit. Ideally, the outcomes $|0\cdots0\rangle$ and $|1\cdots1\rangle$ each have probability 0.5. Because you measure every qubit, an error on any qubit in the chain, including a readout error, reduces the probability of the ideal outcomes. The measured outputs are therefore sensitive to the physical qubits that the transpiler selects.

Refreshed properties tend to matter most at moderate sizes, where the transpiler can choose from many candidate qubit chains. Very large circuits use most of the device, so the transpiler has little room to avoid poorly performing qubits. To save QPU time, reduce the list of circuit sizes.

```python
num_qubits_list = list(range(5, 41, 5))
circuits = []
for num_qubits in num_qubits_list:
    circuit = QuantumCircuit(num_qubits)
    circuit.h(0)
    for qubit in range(num_qubits - 1):
        circuit.cx(qubit, qubit + 1)
    circuit.measure_all()
    circuits.append(circuit)

circuits[1].draw(output="mpl", style="clifford", fold=-1)
```

### Transpile with reported and refreshed properties

The transpiler uses a `Target` to choose physical qubits and native instructions. If you update only the backend properties, an existing target can remain unchanged. Instead, copy the original target and explicitly apply the refreshed errors and coherence times to the copy. The copy keeps the original instruction durations and connectivity. If a coherence time is unavailable for a qubit, keep its existing target value.

A reported error of 1 means that the error is undefined, because benchmarking of that qubit or gate has not succeeded for several days, and the value is outdated (see [QPU information](https://docs.quantum.ibm.com/ibm-quantum-compute/qpu-information)). This value does not necessarily mean that the gate is not working, but you should treat the gate with caution. A single characterization run can still fit a low error for such a gate, so that the gate appears to be one of the best on the device. To be cautious, keep those entries at their reported value so that the transpiler continues to avoid them.

Use the same optimization level and random seed for both compilations so that the comparison controls for transpiler randomness.

```python
target_updated = deepcopy(backend.target)
properties_updated = backend_updated.properties()

for gate in properties_updated.gates:
    if not any(
        parameter.name == "gate_error" for parameter in gate.parameters
    ):
        continue
    qargs = tuple(gate.qubits)
    if gate.gate not in target_updated.operation_names:
        continue
    if qargs not in target_updated[gate.gate]:
        continue
    instruction_properties = deepcopy(target_updated[gate.gate][qargs])
    # Keep gates whose reported error is undefined (error of 1)
    if (
        instruction_properties is not None
        and instruction_properties.error < 1
    ):
        instruction_properties.error = properties_updated.gate_error(
            gate.gate, qargs
        )
        target_updated.update_instruction_properties(
            gate.gate, qargs, instruction_properties
        )

for qubit in range(backend.num_qubits):
    measurement_properties = deepcopy(target_updated["measure"][(qubit,)])
    if measurement_properties.error < 1:
        measurement_properties.error = properties_updated.readout_error(qubit)
        target_updated.update_instruction_properties(
            "measure", (qubit,), measurement_properties
        )
    qubit_properties = properties_updated.qubit_property(qubit)
    if "T1" in qubit_properties:
        target_updated.qubit_properties[qubit].t1 = qubit_properties["T1"][0]
    if "T2" in qubit_properties:
        target_updated.qubit_properties[qubit].t2 = qubit_properties["T2"][0]

pm_reported = generate_preset_pass_manager(
    target=backend.target, optimization_level=3, seed_transpiler=42
)
pm_refreshed = generate_preset_pass_manager(
    target=target_updated, optimization_level=3, seed_transpiler=42
)
isa_reported = pm_reported.run(circuits)
isa_refreshed = pm_refreshed.run(circuits)
```

### Execute both sets of circuits

Run both versions on the same QPU, with the same shot count and error suppression settings. Interleave the reported and refreshed versions in the submission, and repeat the comparison three times to observe the variation between trials. The order of submission does not guarantee the order of execution, and interleaving does not eliminate drift.

```python
n_trials = 3
shots = 4096
interleaved_circuits = [
    circuit for pair in zip(isa_reported, isa_refreshed) for circuit in pair
]
sampler = SamplerV2(mode=backend)
sampler.options.environment.job_tags = ["TUT_RTBQS"]
sampler.options.dynamical_decoupling.enable = True
sampler.options.dynamical_decoupling.sequence_type = "XY4"
comparison_job = sampler.run(interleaved_circuits * n_trials, shots=shots)
```

### Compare the output distributions

Compute the Hellinger fidelity between each measured distribution and the ideal GHZ distribution. A value of 1 means that the distributions match. This metric measures agreement only in the computational basis. It does not measure the coherence between $|0\cdots0\rangle$ and $|1\cdots1\rangle$, so it is an upper bound on the GHZ state fidelity, not a certificate of entanglement.

The plot shows the mean across trials. The error bars indicate one standard deviation.

```python
comparison_results = comparison_job.result()
fidelities = np.empty((n_trials, len(num_qubits_list), 2))
for trial in range(n_trials):
    for chain_index in range(len(num_qubits_list)):
        for variant in range(2):
            result_index = (
                trial * 2 * len(num_qubits_list) + 2 * chain_index + variant
            )
            counts = comparison_results[result_index].data.meas.get_counts()
            num_qubits = num_qubits_list[chain_index]
            ideal_dist = {"0" * num_qubits: 0.5, "1" * num_qubits: 0.5}
            fidelities[trial, chain_index, variant] = hellinger_fidelity(
                ideal_dist, counts
            )

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
for variant, label in enumerate(
    ["Reported properties", "Refreshed properties"]
):
    ax.errorbar(
        num_qubits_list,
        fidelities[:, :, variant].mean(axis=0),
        yerr=fidelities[:, :, variant].std(axis=0),
        fmt="o-",
        label=label,
    )
ax.set_xlabel("Number of qubits in GHZ state")
ax.set_ylabel("Hellinger fidelity")
ax.set_xticks(num_qubits_list, labels=num_qubits_list, rotation=45)
ax.legend()
ax.grid(True)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

for num_qubits, (reported, refreshed) in zip(
    num_qubits_list, fidelities.mean(axis=0)
):
    print(
        f"GHZ-{num_qubits}: reported {reported:.3f} | refreshed {refreshed:.3f}"
    )
```

In this run, the refreshed properties changed some or all of the selected qubits at every size, and the refreshed layouts gave a higher mean fidelity for seven of the eight GHZ states. The largest gain was for the 25-qubit state, where the mean fidelity increased from 0.629 to 0.694. The refreshed layout scored higher in every trial for the 5-, 10-, 20-, 25-, 35-, and 40-qubit states. Not every size improved. For the 15-qubit state, the refreshed layout was slightly worse (0.813 compared with 0.821). For the 30-qubit state, the difference (0.596 compared with 0.591) is within the variation between trials.

Refreshed properties do not guarantee better results. The benefit depends on how far the device has drifted since its last calibration. Also, the characterization is itself a noisy snapshot. The transpiler can select a qubit or gate that happens to measure well, even if that qubit or gate does not perform better. Shot noise, further drift between characterization and execution, and differences in characterization methodology also affect the comparison. Use the measured change and its variation to decide whether refreshed properties are useful for your workload.
